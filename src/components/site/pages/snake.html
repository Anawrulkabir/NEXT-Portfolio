Case study · simulation, not hardware

A compliant snake robot, simulated before it is built.

A 7-segment, cable-driven snake robot that I will build with micro servos and 3D-printed parts. Before buying anything, I built a MuJoCo simulation to test one idea from the literature: a partly compliant body gets through cluttered terrain better than a stiff or a loose one (Wang et al., Science Robotics 2023).

The question

  1. Does intermediate cable compliance beat stiff and loose bodies in a post lattice, under the same open-loop wave?
  2. What does the real robot need: joint torque, cable tension, servo class, segment length?
  3. Is adaptive slack worth building: loosen when jammed, tighten to push?

The robot

Body7 rigid segments, 6 yaw joints, 0.10 m pitch, planar
ActuationOne MG90S micro servo per joint driving a bilateral cable spool (0.2027 N·m stall at 6 V)
GroundA passive wheel under each segment: 1.6 : 1 sideways-to-forward drag, the paper’s ratio
Mass0.038 kg per segment, 0.075 kg head (ESP32 and battery). Placeholders until weighed
ComplianceThe paper’s generalized compliance G, from 0 (stiff) to 1.5 (loose)

MuJoCo render, run 20261017T112843Z_29c5654.

The arena

A hexagonal lattice of 0.103 m posts at the paper’s densities. The robot must cross 1.95 m within 40 gait cycles. Every condition runs from the same randomized starts, so comparisons are paired.

How I worked

  • Decision gates

    Eight points where I chose between written options with trade-offs. Every choice and its reason is logged.

  • Pre-registration

    Pass/fail rules were written down before each run, then applied by code. When the rule says “inconclusive”, the project stops there.

  • Tests that can fail

    133 tests, each shown to fail on a planted fault: a flipped sign, a dropped row, a mistyped config value.

  • Disclosure

    The code was written with Claude Code under my direction. My decisions are logged in docs/decisions.md.

Pipeline

Tagged YAML configs are the one source of truth. Python generates the MuJoCo model, and a parallel, resumable sweep runner writes each run’s config, git commit, JSONL log and Parquet copy. Dependencies are locked with uv, and logged trials replay bit-for-bit in a fresh environment.

What I found

E1 pilot: a floor effect0 successes

160 trials, no success at any compliance. A diagnostic re-run showed every overload came from a post holding a joint near the head. All 84 overloaded trials reproduced exactly.

Calibration step 2Inconclusive

Successes out of 5 for G = 0 to 1.5: 5, 4, 1, 2, 5, 0, 0. The pre-registered outcome was inconclusive, so no setup was adopted.

run 20261017T111457Z_cc9bd78
Is the mid-G stall a propulsion failure?Rejected

On open floor all 40 trials succeeded and mid-G speed matched the ends. In the lattice mid-G again did worst: 8/10 and 6/10, against 10/10 at both ends. The stall needs the posts.

run 20261018T071302Z_d73fb14

Same start, seed 1002: stuck at G = 0.75, passing at G = 1.0.

Hardware decision

The MG90S became the baseline servo and the MG996R was dropped: it still overloaded at G = 0, is 4× heavier, barely fits the segment, and may turn only 120°.

What it taught me

  • An inconclusive result reported honestly is worth more than a tuned success.
  • Writing a criterion down before the data exists exposes bad criteria. One of mine was true by construction at G = 0.
  • Most of the work was ruling out artefacts: start-pose bugs, friction priority, metric artefacts, solver resets.
